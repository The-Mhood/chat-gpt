## 1. The question

Board `ENV_GATES_DEAD_FIELD_OF_CLOTH_V001` (Chapter 7, PROPOSED; review item D9, PENDING CONSULTANT REVIEW). The board shows a numberless field of poles, each with one grey strip hanging straight down, a single pale path, one newer foreground strip, and an ash-grey landscape. The question: is there a documented Igbo practice that the field's burial-cloth reading could rest on?

## 2. Answer in brief

- **No documented pre-colonial or historical Igbo practice of cloth strips hung on free-standing poles as a grave, funerary or memorial marker was found.** The search record in the pack (about 60 earlier searches) and in this session (about 20 further searches, including three targeted at grave posts, funerary cloth and Igbo-Ukwu burial) turned up none. Absence is not proof of non-existence. A systematic regional survey of posts and flags across south-eastern Nigeria has not been done (see U-03).
- **Documented Igbo funerary cloth does exist, but it is used on the body or as a stand-in for the body, not on poles.** This is the strongest new finding, and it is regional.
- **Field of Cloth should therefore not be captioned or framed as an Igbo funerary marker.** If the CD wants a cloth-and-death element, the evidenced forms are (a) a cloth covering, tying or carrying the body, or (b) a cloth standing in for the dead during a procession. Both are documented, both are regional, and both are unlike the board's hung strips.
- Verdict unchanged from C: POTENTIALLY MISLEADING and CULTURALLY SENSITIVE as a whole. Not CULTURALLY SUPPORTED. Not FICTIONAL AND CULTURALLY NEUTRAL as a whole.

### 3.2 Cloth used for sacred trees and shrines (not funerary)

- White cloth on the trunks of some sacred trees: Akpu and Udara, in a secondary article (RP-04, `OKE22`). That article gives **no separate citation for those sentences**. It is devotional, not funerary, and REGIONALLY SPECIFIC. Iroko, Oji and Achi have **no white-cloth statement** in that article.
- Shrine walls hung with cult apparatus (Awka area; cloth not specified): `COA84`.
- The Ogirishi grave-headside and boundary tree: a secondary article gives this statement without a source. **UNVERIFIED** (pack, C-entries and R-26).
- Do **not** treat sacred-tree cloth and documented corpse covering or grave goods as equal in evidence strength (TD FINAL instruction).

### 3.3 Non-Igbo comparisons (labelled; never attributed to Igbo)

- Fante (Cape Coast) asafo flags: AID11 (thesis record and abstract only). Scope is Fante; the early-18th-century origin claim is not verified.
- Yoruba white cloth on sacred trees: APAS snippet (non-Igbo).
- Bongo (Sudan) grave posts: UNVERIFIED (pack).
- Ewe, Jeje and Brazilian vodun cloth-wrapped trees: UNVERIFIED, Tier 4 only.
- Southern African (Nguni) shields and other comparisons: not relevant to the field.

## 5. Relationship to Landing of the Unclaimed (D11)

- Landing has rampart strips on poles (review item, Task 7: "hanging straight vertically"). The same pole-and-strip problem applies. Recommend the same caution: no Igbo attribution of the rampart strips.
- Canoe use: riverine Igbo (Onitsha, Aboh and Anambra-valley communities) used canoes for trade and fishing (RP-51, RP-52, T3/T4). Mainland Igbo are described as less canoe-reliant (RP-51). This is **DISPUTED in detail**, not settled (U-21).
- An Oru-Igbo ferrying rite (RP-33) and the "unclaimed" concept (bad death, RP-32) are both possible readings for a riverside landing. **Either reading would make the landing a funerary scene.** That is a CD decision, not a research output. If the CD chooses a funerary reading, it must be attributed to the named region and consulted.

## 7. Recommendation (researcher; CD decides)

1. **Keep D9 on HOLD** until the consultant review (outside this programme) and a CD framing decision are complete. Research does not clear D9.
2. **Choose a framing (U-02).** Evidence supports three options. The three match C Q10:
   - (a) Keep the landscape fictional, with a non-Igbo framing, and no burial-cloth reading.
   - (b) Keep the fictional landscape inside an Igbo-inspired world, but change the funerary cloth to a clearly invented form that does not resemble documented practice.
   - (c) Keep the landscape, drop the burial-cloth reading, and consult before any burial-associated element.
   Option (b) is the one the evidence most readily supports. It keeps the landscape and avoids claiming a documented pole practice.
3. **If a cloth-and-death element is kept,** prefer a form the evidence documents (a cloth covering or carried with the body) over pole-hung strips. Treat it as regional. Consult before finalising. Do not depict the corpse.
4. **Do not caption the field as Igbo,** and do not add narrative figures or ritual props from RP-31 to RP-33.
5. **Attach the disclaimer** to every use of the field: "Fictional. Not a depiction of any documented Igbo or other African funerary practice."

## 8. What would change this report

- A retrieved primary source (ethnography, archive, museum record) documenting cloth hung on posts or poles at graves or in a funerary field, with a locality and date. Priority search: Basden (1921) and Talbot (1926) full text; Shaw (1970); Ames (1973) is not relevant here.
- A consultant finding, through the separate consultant process, that a specific Igbo community uses such a marker.
- A CD ruling on framing (U-02).

## 9. Honest limits

- Most sources were read as snippets. Full texts of Basden (1921), Shaw (1970) and Cole & Aniakor (1984) were not read.
- Page numbers are not verified except where stated.
- Community variation is large. Udenu, Umumba-Ndiagu, Nsukka and Oru-Igbo are four regions; none of them is "Igbo" as a whole.
- Colonial-era accounts (Basden, Talbot) describe their own period and may reflect colonial reporting. They are used here only as leads.
