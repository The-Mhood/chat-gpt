## Part 1 — Concept as stated on the review board

Board facts (verified from the review image `CD_REVIEW_2026-11-16/REVIEW_B4W_03_FIELD_OF_CLOTH_SIDE_BY_SIDE.jpg`):

- **Title:** "THE GATES OF THE DEAD — THE FIELD OF CLOTH"
- **Project:** THE DISOBEDIENT DAUGHTER / Nwanyi Nwanyoghi Ibe
- **Register:** CH07-SUB-005 PROPOSED · Status: REVIEW REQUIRED
- **Read (intent):** "Death has no hierarchy... each with one strip of grey cloth hanging still. One strip in the foreground is newer than the rest. A single pale path."
- **Rules:** "One strip per pole, hanging straight down. Never strung in lines."
- **Sensitivity:** "Burial imagery needs consultant review. It copies no real grave marker."
- **Known issues:** poles are sparse (should feel numberless); strips are thin and hard to see against the pale ground.
- **Palette:** seven grey swatches.
- **Footer:** "Entirely fictional. This is the game's invented land of the dead and makes no statement about Igbo belief. Design PROPOSED, not canon."

### Important reading of the board
The board itself declares the scene fictional. That framing is useful but does not resolve the issue, for three reasons:
1. The project is Igbo-inspired and the game's audience will read the imagery through that frame. A disclaimer on a review board does not travel with the image.
2. The board's own sensitivity note treats the imagery as **burial imagery**, which is a real concern area in Igbo funerary practice (B.3).
3. "It copies no real grave marker" is a claim the team has made. Desk research supports the narrower statement that **no documented pole-and-cloth grave marker was found**, but cannot establish that no community uses a comparable form. That question is for a consultant.

---

## Part 2 — Verdict on the pole-and-cloth field as a whole

**Working verdict: POTENTIALLY MISLEADING and CULTURALLY SENSITIVE.**

- **Not CULTURALLY SUPPORTED:** no documented pre-colonial Igbo practice matches the arrangement (B.7).
- **Not NOT RECOMMENDED outright:** the evidence does not show that the representation is harmful when it is clearly fictional and detached from Igbo identity. The concern is about framing and reproduction, which a consultant can address.
- **Not FICTIONAL AND CULTURALLY NEUTRAL** as a whole: the imagery is set in an Igbo-inspired project and uses burial-associated material (grey burial cloth, poles, death atmosphere). Neutrality would require that no cultural identity is implied, which is hard to maintain in this project.

The individual elements require separate classification (Part 3).

## Part 3 — Element-by-element assessment

Each element is assessed separately. Multiple classifications apply. Confidence is High / Medium / Low.

### E1 — Vast, austere, ash-grey landscape
- **Classification:** FICTIONAL AND CULTURALLY NEUTRAL (palette and landscape type).
- **Evidence:** Grey and ash-coloured ground is not documented in the sources reviewed as a funerary colour or mourning convention for Igbo communities. White and black are recurrent in some mourning contexts (B.2.3), so the grey palette is not directly tied to those.
- **Caution:** if the grey cloth is treated as burial cloth, the ash-grey field inherits the sensitivity of E3.
- **Confidence:** Medium.

### E2 — Numerous individual poles (intended "numberless")
- **Classification:** HISTORICALLY INSPIRED (poles and posts as ritual and architectural elements) **plus POTENTIALLY MISLEADING** when the poles form a repeated field in a funerary landscape.
- **Evidence:** Shrine figures, house posts, ancestral figures and Nsude-type shrines are documented (B.4; V.1; V.9). No documented Igbo practice places a numberless field of poles around the dead. Nsude pyramids are shrines, not poles, and bear no cloth.
- **Caution:** a repeated field of posts with cloth is closest to West African pole-flag traditions (e.g., Fante asafo), which are not Igbo (B.7.3).
- **Confidence:** Medium for the poles as elements; Low for the field arrangement.

### E3 — One vertically hanging grey cloth strip per pole
- **Classification:** POTENTIALLY MISLEADING and CULTURALLY SENSITIVE.
- **Evidence:**
  - Not documented in the sources reviewed as a pre-colonial Igbo funerary, mourning or memorial marker (B.7.2).
  - The closest documented Igbo cloth-on-structure practice is singular white cloth on a sacred tree trunk (devotional, REGIONALLY SPECIFIC) [APAS — VERIFY].
  - Cloth as grave goods and corpse covering is documented (REGIONALLY SPECIFIC) [IGI25]. Grey cloth is not described in these accounts; black cloth is.
- **Caution:** the strip is a **burial-cloth** motif in the board's own wording. A viewer may read it as a grave goods or corpse-cloth reference. That is a claim about Igbo burial, which is not supported by evidence for this form.
- **Confidence:** High that no documented equivalent was found; Medium that the reading as burial cloth is a risk; a consultant is required to confirm sensitivity.

### E4 — Never strung between poles (cloth not joined)
- **Classification:** FICTIONAL AND CULTURALLY NEUTRAL (a stylistic rule).
- **Evidence:** No documented Igbo practice either requires or prohibits cloth being strung between posts. The rule is an artistic constraint, not an observed custom.
- **Caution:** the rule should not be presented as a religious prohibition, since no Igbo prohibition on this has been documented.
- **Confidence:** High.

### E5 — Single pale path through the field
- **Classification:** INFERRED / FICTIONAL. Potentially suggestive of a journey to the afterlife.
- **Evidence:** Some Igbo communities describe a journey or the ancestral world under the earth (II.5). The path form itself is not documented.
- **Caution:** a path is a common afterlife motif in many cultures. If it is drawn as a specifically Igbo route, that is **SPECULATIVE**.
- **Confidence:** Low.

### E6 — One newer-looking foreground strip (the focal signal)
- **Classification:** FICTIONAL ARTISTIC DEVICE in intent; **CULTURALLY SENSITIVE** in effect.
- **Evidence:**
  - Fresh cloth on a corpse or grave is documented in terms of corpse covering and grave goods (B.3). A newer-looking strip can therefore be read as a reference to a recent death.
  - No source reviewed documents a newer cloth strip on a pole as a marker of recent death. **NOT FOUND.**
- **Caution:** the board frames this as the "newest" death — "Death has no hierarchy" is contradicted by the single foreground strip being given visual priority. This is an interpretive problem as well as a sensitivity problem, and it could present a grief narrative that implies a real burial custom.
- **Historically defensible?** Not as a documented practice. It can be retained as a storytelling device only if it is clearly fictional and is not presented as a real funerary marker.
- **Confidence:** High that no documented equivalent exists; Medium on the sensitivity effect.

### E7 — Death and boundary atmosphere ("gates of the dead")
- **Classification:** FICTIONAL, with **POTENTIALLY MISLEADING** risk if read as Igbo afterlife.
- **Evidence:** Death classes, bad death, second burial, and ancestral worlds are documented (B.1). A tree marker (the Ogirishi, reported as marking grave headsides and boundaries; Oha 2019 via Ochendo; VERIFY) is a documented boundary-related grave element, though it is a tree rather than poles or cloth. No single Igbo "gate" or boundary landscape is documented. The concept of a land of the dead varies by community and is **DISPUTED**.
- **Caution:** a "Gates of the Dead" title asserts a fixed boundary structure. Use "gates" or "land of the dead" only as fictional terms, and do not attach them to Igbo religion.
- **Confidence:** Medium.

### E8 — Overall composition as a "land of the dead" (footer disclaimer)
- **Classification:** FICTIONAL, with the disclaimer valid only if it travels with the image and title.
- **Evidence:** the disclaimer "makes no statement about Igbo belief" is a commitment the team must hold to in all uses (marketing, art book, in-game text).
- **Confidence:** High that the disclaimer is necessary; Medium that it is sufficient.

## Part 5 — The twelve assessment questions

### Q1. Is there reliable evidence for comparable pole-and-cloth arrangements in pre-colonial Igbo societies?
**No**, not in the sources reviewed (55–60 searches, count approximate). Closest documented practices are singular cloth on sacred-tree trunks (REGIONALLY SPECIFIC), cloth grave goods and corpse coverings (REGIONALLY SPECIFIC), and shrine walls with cult apparatus (Awka area). [B.7.2]
**Confidence:** Medium (search-based; absence not proof).

### Q2. Are cloth strips, hanging textiles, poles or posts documented in Igbo funerary or mourning practices?
- **Cloth:** yes, as grave goods and corpse covering (REGIONALLY SPECIFIC) [IGI25].
- **Hanging textiles:** on sacred trees and shrine walls, not on funerary poles (REGIONALLY SPECIFIC).
- **Poles/posts:** yes, as shrine figures and house posts; not documented as funerary markers with cloth.
**Confidence:** Medium.

### Q3. If comparable arrangements exist, what were their actual purposes?
- **Sacred-tree cloth:** devotional; shrine or lineage honour. Not funerary in the sources reviewed.
- **Shrine-wall cult apparatus:** shrine and ritual objects; restricted.
- **Ukara (Cross River Igbo, Ekpe):** cloth in meeting houses; restricted and tied to a society. Tier 4 lead; VERIFY.
**Confidence:** Low–Medium.

### Q4. Were they associated with burial, mourning, memorialisation, title-taking, religious ceremonies or something else?
- Sacred-tree cloth: religious / devotional.
- Grave goods and corpse covering: burial.
- Shrine-wall apparatus: religious and ritual.
- Ukara: society and title-related (Ekpe).
- No documented association with memorial poles.
**Confidence:** Medium.

### Q5. Were they restricted to particular communities, social groups, genders or circumstances?
**Yes.** All identified practices are REGIONALLY SPECIFIC. Titled men's grave goods are restricted by status. Ekpe and ukara are restricted to a society. Sacred-tree practice is community-specific. Masquerade and initiation material is gender- and initiation-restricted.
**Confidence:** High that restrictions exist; Medium on their details.

### Q6. Could the proposed imagery unintentionally resemble a sacred or restricted practice?
**Yes.** Two candidates:
1. **Sacred-tree white cloth:** the field's cloth-on-vertical-structure could be read as a version of this devotional practice, which is sacred. Colour and form differ, but a viewer may connect them.
2. **Grave goods and corpse covering:** a cloth on each grave-like marker could be read as a restricted burial practice of titled men, which is sacred to the families.
**Confidence:** Medium.

### Q7. Could the imagery be mistaken for another African culture's funerary tradition?
**Yes, with a high probability.** Pole-and-cloth arrangements are strongly associated with:
- **Fante asafo flags (Ghana):** cloth on poles, a famous West African pole-flag tradition.
- **Ewe, Jeje and Brazilian vodun:** cloth-wrapped trees.
- **Bongo grave posts (Sudan).**
A viewer familiar with these may attribute the field to a different tradition, and an Igbo-labelled game would then misattribute it.
**Confidence:** High that the resemblance is real; Medium on how likely viewers are to make the connection.

### Q8. Does the arrangement create a misleading impression of a universal Igbo burial custom?
**Yes, if presented as Igbo.** The board frames it as invented, but a numberless field of grey burial cloth in an Igbo-inspired game can be read as a generalised Igbo funerary landscape. This would reduce the diversity of Igbo burial practice (titled versus untitled, regional, bad death versus respected death) to a single image.
**Confidence:** High.

### Q9. Is the single newer foreground cloth historically defensible, or is it purely an artistic storytelling device?
**It is an artistic storytelling device.** No documented Igbo practice marks a recent death with a newer cloth strip on a pole (NOT FOUND). The closest real element is fresh cloth as a corpse covering or grave good, which is a different form. As a story device it is defensible if it is clearly fictional and does not imply a real custom. As a visual signal of grief for a recent death, it implies a funerary practice that the evidence does not support.
**Confidence:** High that no documented equivalent exists.

### Q10. Can the visual concept be retained as an explicitly fictional landscape without claiming historical authenticity?
**Conditionally yes, but not as an Igbo-attributed funerary landscape.** Options that the research does not decide (CD decision):
- (a) Keep as fictional and clearly non-Igbo in naming and framing, with no Igbo attribution in any text or marketing.
- (b) Keep as fictional within the Igbo-inspired world but change the funerary cloth motif to a clearly invented form that does not resemble documented Igbo, Asafo, or vodun practice.
- (c) Retain the landscape, drop the burial-cloth reading entirely, and consult before any burial-associated element is used.
Under any option, the disclaimer must travel with the image.
**Confidence:** Medium.

### Q11. What changes, if any, would make the design more culturally responsible?
1. Remove the burial-cloth framing from the sensitivity and read notes (the sensitivity should be about the consultant question, not a settled claim).
2. Do not treat the newer foreground strip as a recent-death marker.
3. Avoid a numberless field of cloth-bearing poles in any Igbo-labelled context.
4. Avoid grey strips that closely resemble grave-goods cloth or corpse covering in form.
5. Do not use sacred-tree white cloth as a visual source.
6. Attach the disclaimer to every use, including titles and captions.
7. Decide, as CD, whether the world is Igbo-inspired or Igbo-attributed; this changes the risk.
8. Consult before any revision (Deliverable F).
**Confidence:** Medium–High.

### Q12. What specific questions must be put to a living Igbo cultural custodian before the design can proceed?
See **Deliverable F**. The most important are: (i) whether any community uses cloth strips on posts or poles near burial or shrine sites; (ii) whether grey cloth has funerary meaning; (iii) whether a newer cloth marks a recent death; (iv) whether a fictional land of the dead is acceptable to depict; (v) whether sacred-tree or ukara imagery is restricted.

## Part 6 — Final recommendation (answers to the seven §7 questions)

**Disclaimer:** these are researcher recommendations. They do not approve the artwork on behalf of the Creative Director. The decision remains with the Creative Director.

### 6.1 What can we confidently represent as historically grounded in pre-colonial Igbo culture?
- Community-specific ancestral and shrine structures (obi/obu/ovu halls, shrine walls, shrine figures) in general terms, with regional labels. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Grave-related cloth as a general material fact: cloth was interred with the dead or used to cover the body in some communities. Use only with regional labels. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Bast-fibre and ribbon-weave textiles (Igbo-Ukwu evidence), with no silk. **DOCUMENTED.**
- Iron-working and smithing, bronze work at Igbo-Ukwu (with the dating dispute noted). **DOCUMENTED.**
- The four-day market week as a general cultural fact. **CORROBORATED.**
- Ogene and ekwe as musical instruments. **CORROBORATED.**

### 6.2 What should we avoid representing?
- Any cloth strip hung individually from a pole as an Igbo funerary, mourning or memorial marker. **NOT DOCUMENTED.**
- A numberless field of cloth-bearing poles attributed to Igbo culture.
- A newer cloth strip as a marker of a specific recent death.
- Sacred-tree white cloth as a visual source.
- Ikenga, ichi, ozo regalia, masquerade costume, nsibidi signs, uli shrine imagery, and mbari imagery, without custodian consultation.
- Silk textiles for pre-colonial Igbo.
- Osu, twin, ogbanje and bad-death imagery, without custodian consultation.
- Any claim that a single Igbo "land of the dead" exists.

### 6.3 What is acceptable as fictional worldbuilding?
- An invented land of the dead, clearly fictional, with a non-Igbo or invented name and framing, and with no statement about Igbo belief.
- Invented funerary objects that do not copy documented Igbo or other African practices. (Note: "invented" must still avoid resemblance to asafo flags, vodun trees, Bongo posts, or sacred-tree cloth; otherwise the risk shifts.)
- Ash-grey and pale palettes as aesthetic choices, not as claimed Igbo colours.
- A pale path as an invented device, not as a documented Igbo route.

### 6.4 What requires further academic research?
- Nsukka-region funerary and shrine practice.
- Pole, post and flag use across south-eastern Nigeria (a systematic search, including primary ethnographies not yet reviewed).
- Whether any Igbo community uses cloth on posts near graves or compounds (a targeted ethnographic search).
- Igbo-Ukwu dating and identity (DISPUTED).
- Chukwu and chi concepts (DISPUTED).
- Child burial treatment (GAP).
- Verification of the [VERIFY] sources listed in `G_Bibliography.md`.

### 6.5 What requires a living Igbo cultural consultant?
- All burial-associated imagery, including cloth, graves and any "gate" or land-of-the-dead motif.
- Whether the newer-cloth motif implies recent death in any community.
- Restricted items: ikenga, ichi, ozo, masquerades, nsibidi, mbari, uli shrine imagery, ukara.
- Regional specificity: which communities, if any, would be affected by this design.
- Whether a fictional land of the dead, within an Igbo-inspired game, is acceptable.
- Child and bad death imagery.

### 6.6 Can the Field of Cloth concept proceed to visual revision?
**Not yet.** Recommendation: **HOLD visual revision of the burial-cloth elements (E3, E6) until the consultant review (D9) is complete.** The following may proceed in planning without producing final artwork:
- Non-funerary structural study (e.g., the ash-grey landscape and a generic path) — with the caveat that the path and palette are fictional.
- A documented list of questions to the consultant (Deliverable F).

Visual revision of the pole-and-cloth field itself should not proceed on this evidence. The research does not block a fictional landscape in principle; it blocks a burial-cloth field attributed to Igbo culture.

### 6.7 If not, what precisely must be resolved first?
1. **Consultant review (D9 gate):** a qualified Igbo cultural custodian must answer Deliverable F, particularly questions on pole-and-cloth practice, grey cloth, newer-cloth meaning, and the fictional land of the dead.
2. **Creative Director framing decision:** decide whether the world is Igbo-inspired, Igbo-attributed, or a separate invented culture with Igbo-inspired visual language. This is a CD decision, not a research decision, and it changes the risk level.
3. **Burial-cloth element decision:** decide whether the grey strip is a burial-cloth reference. If it is, the consultant must clear it; if not, the element should be redesigned to avoid burial-cloth resemblance.
4. **Newer foreground strip decision:** remove the recent-death reading or confirm, with the consultant, that it is acceptable as fiction.
5. **Verification of sources:** confirm the [VERIFY] sources cited in this assessment before they are used in any external-facing material.
6. **Disclaimer policy:** the footer disclaimer must be carried into every use, and the team must confirm this as a production requirement.

**Summary for the Creative Director:** the research does not find a documented Igbo pole-and-cloth funerary practice. The concept's individual elements have partial references, but the whole reads as a burial landscape in an Igbo-inspired project. The concept is **POTENTIALLY MISLEADING and CULTURALLY SENSITIVE**. The evidence supports keeping it on hold until the consultant review is done and the framing decision is made.
