# Deliverable B — Funerary and Ancestral Practices Report
THE DISOBEDIENT DAUGHTER · Pre-colonial Igbo-speaking communities · Working date 2026-11-17

> **Headline:** No documented pre-colonial Igbo practice was found in which cloth strips hang individually from free-standing poles as a funerary, mourning, memorial or spiritual marker. The reported cloth-and-death practices differ in form: cloth as grave goods and a black cloth covering the corpse (both from one community's account); a white cloth on the trunk of some sacred trees (one secondary report only); and cult apparatus on Awka-area shrine walls (cloth not specified). Evidence is **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** throughout. Absence in the sources reviewed is not proof of absence.

---

## B.1 Death and the afterlife

### B.1.1 Classes of death
Several communities distinguished:
- **Ordinary death** — the expected death of an adult.
- **Respected death** — the death of a titled elder or a person of high status, often with elaborate rites (see B.2).
- **Premature death** — death of a young person or a child; treatment varies by community.
- **Bad death (ajo ofia)** — including suicide, some epidemic diseases (e.g., smallpox, "swelling" disease), twin-related deaths, the death of an osu, and deaths linked to particular taboos. The dead in these categories could be denied normal rites or buried outside the compound. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** [BJM25 — VERIFY; BAS21; BAS66 p.114 as cited in BJM25; ACH58 — cultural trope only].

### B.1.2 Transition from person to ancestor
- Ancestor status depended on a proper death and funerary completion. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** Second burial (see B.2.4) is one completion mechanism.
- The ancestor's relationship with the living is maintained through lineage shrines, libations and naming. **CORROBORATED** across regions [UCH65; BAS21].

### B.1.3 Afterlife concepts
- Several communities described the dead as continuing life in an ancestral world (ala mmuo / the spirit realm), sometimes located under the earth. The details — location, journey, judgement — are **DISPUTED** and **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC**.
- **No source reviewed describes a single, fixed landscape of the dead** that could be used as a visual template. Any visual "land of the dead" is therefore **FICTIONAL** unless grounded in a named community practice. (See Deliverable C.)

### B.1.4 Reincarnation and returning ancestors
- Ancestral return through a newborn child is widely attested in naming practices. **CORROBORATED.**
- Ogbanje (children who die repeatedly and return) and the iyi-uwa object are documented in several communities. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC; iyi-uwa details restricted.**

## B.2 Burial and mourning

### B.2.1 Burial preparation
- A dying titled man was washed and prepared by specified persons. An account from Umumba-Ndiagu (Chinweze, *Igirigi* 2025 — author and title confirmed in session; page numbers VERIFY) describes the body washed with a pomade (elu aki), covered with black cloth, with a cap bearing an eagle feather and red feather, and a titled staff placed in the hand; Ozo men supervised. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** to that account's community.
- The first son identified the grave site in that account. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** *Not confirmed in the retrieved text — VERIFY.*

### B.2.2 Grave construction and location
- **Titled men** were buried within their own compound or obi in several communities, not in a communal cemetery [IGI25; ONW81]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- At Obimo (Nsukka area), the Ozo man's grave is reported as dug in **two chambers** (one for the corpse; a second described as "for his bed" in one retrieved version, while the Igirigi text is garbled at that point). This detail traces to Ugwuoke (1998) as reported in later articles, so it is one lineage of reporting, not independent corroboration. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC; VERIFY.**
- A grave-lining with wooden planks is reported in Tier 4 leads [REM (remembersgroup)]. **UNVERIFIED** — do not use as fact.
- The Igbo Richard burial at Igbo-Ukwu (wood-lined chamber; seated figure on studded stool; the ~8 m depth appears only in a Tier 4 summary and is unverified) is a major **archaeological** case of elaborate burial. Its identity and date are **DISPUTED** (see Dossier I.2). [SHA70]

### B.2.3 Funeral ceremonies and mourning
- Funerals involved music (ogene, ekwe, igba), dance, masquerades in some communities, and feasting. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** [GAR20 — Tier 4; OTT75]
- Masquerades connected with funerals and the dead in some Nsukka-area and central-area traditions (e.g., Odo/Omabe; mmonwu). **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC and restricted** [ONY — VERIFY].
- **Widowhood practices** (shaving the head, restricted diet, sleeping conditions, wearing the same garments for a period, ritual washing with the water used for the husband's body) are documented in **CONTEMPORARY** and early-to-mid 20th-century accounts. Their form, extent and regional distribution are **DISPUTED**; some communities have abandoned or modified them [IRB00 — general Nigerian widowhood, not Igbo-specific; MAD16 — Tier 4; ECO00]. **Do not present these as pre-colonial Igbo universals.**
- The Kalabari and Ijaw descriptions (widow shaving, cloths, a funeral compound) found in a student essay are **not Igbo** and are not used as evidence for Igbo practice [KAL90 — VERIFY].

### B.2.4 Second burial (ikwa ozu / ime ekwa / ili ozu)
- Second burial is a funerary completion held after the first burial, with timing varying by community. In the Nawfia account, about one year, with a formal event at izu ato (12 days) [UGW — ajol.info, VERIFY].
- In Ezzaland practice (Igwebuike account), ili ozu and ime ekwa are distinguished [EZZ — VERIFY].
- Basden noted that "second burial" was a term adopted by Europeans. [BAS21; BAS66 p.114 via BJM25] **This means the English phrase is a colonial-era label**; local terms should be used.
- **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** Not all Igbo communities practise a second burial in the same form.

### B.2.5 Relationships between the dead, family and community
- The eldest son and lineage elders had defined roles in burial. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** [IGI25]
- Community responsibilities in funerals (e.g., contributions, feast obligations) are documented in many accounts. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

### B.2.6 Age, gender, status and title
- Titled and untitled men were buried differently; titled men had more elaborate rites and grave goods. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [IGI25].
- Women's funerary roles, including umuada, are documented. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [AMA87; ILO].
- Children and young people who died were treated differently; in some communities they received minimal rites. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC and under-researched — GAP.**

### B.2.7 Children and young people
- Children's deaths and ogbanje beliefs are documented (B.1.4). Their burial treatment varies by community. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** Any visual representation of child death must be considered highly sensitive and should be consulted. **GAP** in comparative data.

## B.3 Textiles and funerary objects

### B.3.1 Cloth as grave goods
- Cloth was interred with the dead; its quantity was measured in fathoms; a figure of twelve fathoms for a father is reported in one secondary account; it was not confirmed in the retrieved 2025 text — VERIFY. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- The best wrappers were used in funerary contexts in some communities. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Black cloth covering the corpse is cited in the same account. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

### B.3.2 Cloth in the grave (archaeology)
- Two British Museum fabric samples from Igbo-Ukwu, examined by McIntosh & Cartwright (2022), were identified as bast fibres from *Ficus* (fig) species combined with leaf fibres from *Raphia* palm. The findings concern these two samples only and must not be generalised to every Igbo textile tradition. No silk was reported in them. **DOCUMENTED for these two samples.** [SPR22]

### B.3.3 Personal objects buried with the dead
- **Ikenga:** the carved personal object was ritually broken and buried with its owner; it was not inherited. Achebe is cited for the idea that breaking another person's ikenga was a grave offence. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** [AFI86 (via JCRT 2024); SMA (Smarthistory); ACH58 — literary only].
- Tools, weapons and possessions were interred with some men, according to Tier 4 leads [REM]. **UNVERIFIED** in scholarly sources — do not use.

### B.3.4 Stools, bronzes and other status objects
- The Igbo Richard burial includes a studded stool and bronze regalia (SHA70). **DOCUMENTED** as archaeology; **DISPUTED** as to identity.

### B.3.5 Summary
Cloth as funerary goods is real and documented, but it is **interred** with the body or **laid over** the body. It is not documented as strips hung on poles in the field around graves.

## B.4 Ancestors and remembrance

- **Ogirishi tree:** A secondary source reports an Ogirishi tree associated with grave headsides and boundaries; the original source and locality remain unverified. See B.7.2. UNVERIFIED.
- Ancestor shrines and halls (obu/obi/ovu/obiri), libations, naming, and second burial are the main mechanisms. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [OZI25 — Tier 4; BAS21].
- Mbari houses honour Ala and the ancestral world in Owerri-area communities. Their figure-filled walls and ritual content are **restricted**. [COL82 — Tier 2]
- Shrine sculptures in Awka-area shrines show gods and supporters against walls "often hung with cult apparatus" [COA84 p.89]. The apparatus is not described as strips on poles.
- The Nsude pyramids (ten circular stepped mud shrines to Uto) are documented in G.I. Jones photographs of the 1930s. They are **architectural shrines**, not cloth-bearing structures, and reportedly destroyed around 1962 [JON84; MAA]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

### B.7.3 Comparable practices in other African traditions (NOT Igbo)
These are reported elsewhere (scope varies by item; see each bullet) and **must not be attributed to Igbo culture**:
- **Fante (Cape Coast) asafo flags, Ghana:** documented as company flags in Aidoo (2011), an MA thesis (KNUST) on Cape Coast flags since 2000. Its scope is flag documentation and significance. The retrieved record does not establish pole mounting or any funerary use. **Not Igbo; scope limited to Cape Coast.** [AID11]
- **Yoruba:** white cloth tied around large sacred trees to mark them as abodes of deities or sacrifice spots (APAS-YOR, OAAJIS_1_1_8, retrieved as a search snippet; Yoruba (region not stated in the snippet)). **Not Igbo.** Reported Igbo cases (B.7.2) are separate, also secondary, and must not be read as the same practice.
- **Ewe, Jeje and Brazilian vodun:** cloth-wrapped trees, reported in this package only in a Tier 4 Brazilian text. **UNVERIFIED. Not Igbo.** Keep unverified until scholarly sources are obtained.
- **Bongo grave posts (Sudan):** no source in this package. **UNVERIFIED. Not Igbo.**

**Risk:** a viewer familiar with asafo flags or vodun trees may read a pole-and-cloth field as a different West African tradition. This misattribution risk is **Medium–High** if the game is marketed as Igbo-inspired.

### B.7.4 Analysis
- **The sacred-tree white cloth is the closest reported Igbo cloth-on-structure devotional practice (secondary source only).** It differs in form (one tree, one cloth wrapped around the trunk), meaning (devotional/shrine, not burial), and social location (community shrine, as the one secondary article describes it).
- **The grave-goods and corpse-covering practices are the closest cloth-and-death practices reported in the sources reviewed, but both rest on one community's account (IGI25; VERIFY).** They differ in form (cloth buried with or over the body, not on poles above the field) and place (within the compound or grave).
- **The Field of Cloth arrangement combines** a sacred-tree-like form (cloth on a single vertical structure; the sacred-tree report is secondary only), a grave-goods-like material (grey cloth), and a funerary atmosphere, in a repeated field of structures that no documented Igbo practice matches.
- This combination is **FICTIONAL** as a whole. Individual elements have **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** Igbo references (see Deliverable C).

### B.7.5 Overall funerary finding for pole-and-cloth imagery
- **Not documented** as a pre-colonial Igbo funerary practice.
- **Reported** elements exist (sacred-tree cloth, secondary source only; grave-goods cloth, one community's account; shrine-wall cult apparatus, with cloth not specified), but they do not establish the pole-field form.
- Using the pole-field as a representation of Igbo death would be a **misleading cultural claim**.

## B.8 Conclusions for funerary representation

1. Igbo funerary cloth is reported in the sources reviewed in two forms that are **not of equal evidence strength**: (a) **grave goods and corpse covering**, both from one community's account (IGI25; VERIFY; REGIONALLY SPECIFIC); and (b) **sacred-tree devotion**, reported in one secondary article; the cloth sentences carry no separate citation there, and the cited primary source (ADI08) was not retrieved (OKE22). Both should be treated with care.
2. The pole-and-cloth field has no documented Igbo equivalent in the sources reviewed.
3. Any representation of burial or death imagery should be consulted with a living custodian before reproduction (Deliverable F).
4. Igbo death practices vary by community, class and gender. A single "Igbo funeral" image must not be used.
5. Second burial, bad death, ikenga and masquerade-related death practices are restricted or sensitive and need consultation.

---

## B.9 Gaps and limitations
- Children's funerary treatment, pottery and basket funerary use, and Nsukka-area funerals are under-researched.
- Several funerary accounts were drawn from Tier 4 or unverified sources and are flagged **[VERIFY]**.
- Colonial-era sources (Basden, Talbot, Thomas) recorded practices that may have changed by the time of recording. Their dates should be considered when using them.
