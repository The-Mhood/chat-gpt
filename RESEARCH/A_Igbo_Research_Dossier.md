# Deliverable A — Igbo Historical and Cultural Research Dossier
THE DISOBEDIENT DAUGHTER · Pre-colonial Igbo culture · Working date 2026-11-17

> Scope: pre-colonial Igbo-speaking societies of south-eastern Nigeria, with colonial and contemporary material marked as such. Labels follow the mandatory scheme (see `00_README.md`). Source codes map to `G_Bibliography.md`.

---

## 0. Ground rules for using this dossier

1. **"Igbo" is not one culture.** The Igbo-speaking region comprised many communities with different political systems, title structures, burial customs, masquerade institutions, markets and deities. Almost every statement below is tied to a named region or community. Where it is not, the label says so.
2. **Pre-colonial ≠ colonial-era documentation.** Most detailed ethnographic records were made between roughly 1900 and 1940, by colonial officers and missionaries. They are valuable but shaped by their authors (see Part VII).
3. **Absence of a source is not proof of absence.** Several topics below are thinly documented. That is recorded as a gap, not as evidence that a practice did not exist.
4. **Desk research cannot replace a living custodian.** Restricted knowledge (masquerade secrets, shrine practices, initiation, ikenga, ichi, ozo) must not be reproduced on the basis of published accounts alone.

## Part I — Historical foundations

### I.1 Origins and development of Igbo-speaking populations
- Igbo-speaking populations occupy the south-east of present-day Nigeria; their historical depth is debated. Archaeology, linguistic reconstruction and oral history do not yet give a single agreed narrative. **DISPUTED** [ISI76; SHA70] [ORIGIN-UNCERTAIN].
- Oral traditions of origin (e.g., Nri and other communities' origin stories) are **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** and should be treated as community memory, not chronology [MAC09 — is reported in the Nsibidi literature as an Igbo origin legend; NOT confirmed in session — VERIFY; treat as one tradition only].
- Claims that Igbo communities were "ancient" in a single sense, or that they shared one origin, are **DISPUTED**.

### I.2 Archaeology: Igbo-Ukwu and the Nsukka region
- **Igbo-Ukwu** (Anambra) produced bronze and copper-alloy objects, including the Igbo Richard burial: a wood-lined chamber with a seated body, a studded stool, a bronze diadem and a carnelian bead. Excavated by Thurstan Shaw; reported in SHA70 and SHA77 (Tier 1/2). **DOCUMENTED.**
- **Dating is DISPUTED.** The standard reading places the main Igbo-Ukwu deposits around the ninth century; radiocarbon results include a later outlier, and recent reanalyses continue the debate [SHA70; SCI26 (Tier 4 lead)]. Do not state a single date.
- **Identity of the Igbo Richard figure is DISPUTED.** Shaw's identification as an Eze Nri (sacred king) is contested; an alternative reading is an ozo titleholder [SHA70; ONW81].
- **Textiles (Igbo-Ukwu):** McIntosh & Cartwright's 2022 AMS and SEM fibre study (*African Archaeological Review* 39: 405–418; DOI 10.1007/s10437-022-09502-9) of two British Museum fabric samples (scope: these two samples only; not generalisable to Igbo textiles as a whole) found bast fibres from *Ficus* (fig tree) species combined with leaf fibres from *Raphia* palm, indicating a complex indigenous weaving technology, with selvedges and seams. **No silk has been confirmed.** Earlier 1960s analysis could not identify the plant fibres. Do not describe Igbo-Ukwu cloth as silk. **DOCUMENTED** (*Ficus* bast and *Raphia* leaf fibre, SEM identification) [SPR22].
- **Nsukka region:** this package has not yet reviewed Nsukka-region archaeology in depth. **GAP** — see Part VIII.
- **Iron:** a 2024 study reports iron smelting at Lejja at roughly 2000 years before present [LEJ24 — Tier 2, single study; **CORROBORATED** status pending a second independent study]. A claim of smelting at Opi from about 750 BC was found only in Tier 4 leads and is **SPECULATIVE** here.

### I.3 Settlement patterns and community organisation
- Settlement was commonly organised around compounds (ụlọ / obi-type houses), lineage clusters (umunna) and village groups. Compound and hall (obi / obu-type) forms vary by region [BAS21; UCH65; OZI25 (Tier 4)]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** in terms and forms.
- Titled men (ozo and related titles) commonly held compound-based status and were buried within their own compound or obi in several communities. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [IGI25; ONW81].

### I.4 Lineage, kinship and extended family
- Patrilineal descent and lineage (umunna) were widespread, with significant variation: some communities reckoned descent through women in ways that shaped inheritance and marriage. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- The **umuada** (married daughters of a community) and **Omu** (a female-titled figure in some Onitsha-area communities) show that women held political and ritual roles in parts of the region. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [AMA87; ILO (Tier 2 lead)].

### I.5 Political organisation and authority
- The older "acephalous" (headless, non-centralised) model is **DISPUTED**. It was promoted in colonial-era writing and critiqued by later historians who document kingship and centralised institutions in places such as Nri, Onitsha and the Aro confederacy [AFI72; HEN72; ONW81].
- **Nri:** a ritual and political polity with a sacred ruler (Eze Nri) whose ritual-cleansing role extended across a region. The title's historical reach is **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** and the nature of the authority is **DISPUTED** [ONW81; BAS21].
- **Aro confederacy / Ibini Ukpabi:** a trading and oracle network operating across much of the region in the 18th–19th centuries. Its oracular role and commercial power are **DOCUMENTED** in colonial and trade accounts [AFI72; ISI76]; its moral authority over communities is **DISPUTED** in interpretation.
- **Onitsha:** kingship and its court traditions are documented in Henderson's study [HEN72] (**REGIONALLY SPECIFIC**).
- **Ozo:** a titled ritual and social status in many communities, with graded ceremonies. Its meaning, access and prestige vary widely. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** Its detailed ritual content is **restricted** (see Part VI.9).

### I.6 Social hierarchy, titles and age grades
- Titles (ozo, and others in other communities), age-grade associations and lineage seniority structured social status. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- **Osu** (a stigmatised status of persons dedicated to deities or excluded from ordinary kinship) is a real institution. Its origin is **DISPUTED** — accounts variously attribute it to sacrifice, sanctuary-seeking, or dispersal by slave-trading networks. Basden's account is sympathetic but dated [BAS21; BJM25].
- Achebe's literary portrayal of ozo-type titles and of osu is not ethnography and should not be treated as documentary evidence [ACH58].

### I.7 Trade, agriculture, crafts and economy
- **Yam** cultivation and the new yam festival are central to many communities; yam-related festivals are **CORROBORATED** across the region [AHI (Tier 4 lead); UCH65].
- **Markets:** the four-day market week (Eke, Orie, Afọ, Nkwọ) is widely attested. Communities coordinated market days across trading regions; the calendar's historical antiquity and exact regional variation are **DISPUTED** in detail [CAL (Wikipedia, Tier 4); TF22 (Tier 2, citing Ọnụkawa 1998)]. Market days carried religious associations in some communities; these are **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC**.
- **Smithing:** Awka and surrounding communities had itinerant and resident smiths; Neaher's 1979 study documents itinerant smiths **DOCUMENTED** [NEA79].
- **Akwete cloth:** a documented weaving tradition, but dated to the 19th century (not pre-colonial in the sense of the deep past) and associated with Akwete women's cloth [AKW (Tier 4 lead); verify].

### I.8 Warfare, conflict and diplomacy
- Ohafia and Abam communities were known for war-dance complexes and head-taking traditions; the iri agha / Ogbu-Isi institutions and related objects are **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** and **restricted** in parts [OHA (Tier 4 leads, Wikipedia; newsnig)].
- **Ekumeku movement (1883–1914):** anti-colonial guerrilla resistance in the Anioma area (Aniocha, Owa, Kwale), organised through oath-bound networks (otu okorobia). **DOCUMENTED** in Ohadike's study [OHA91 — **VERIFY** full publisher details]. Secondary web summaries were found unreliable and are not used for facts.
- Diplomacy and conflict resolution: oracles, elders' councils, markets and ritual oaths all appear in the literature. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

### I.9 Marriage, family, childhood and adulthood
- Marriage stages commonly include: knocking at the door (iku aka n'uzo), consent of the lineage (umunna), bride-wealth negotiation (ime ego), wine-carrying (igba nkwu) and taking the bride home (idu uno). **CORROBORATED** across several ethnographies, though names and sequences vary [ETH20; MGB22; LEI39; SHO78 — VERIFY].
- Bride-wealth is generally understood as **symbolic and relational** rather than a purchase. Leith-Ross reports women valuing it as dignity and legitimacy [LEI39]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- **Ogbanje** (children believed to die repeatedly and return) and ** iyi-uwa** (the object associated with the cycle) are documented in Achebe's fiction and in ethnographic reports [BAS21; ACH58 for the cultural trope only]. Their interpretation varies. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC**; the iyi-uwa practice itself is **restricted** in details.
- **Twins:** Igbo communities had distinct beliefs about twins, including the umu ejime idea in parts of the Onitsha area. Historical infanticide claims are **DISPUTED**: the "twin-killing" image has been shown to be partly a missionary and colonial construction [ETH-ONI (Gale A75373579)]. Practice in the Calabar/Efik region (Mary Slessor, 1876–1915; criminalised 1906) should not be attributed to Igbo communities [SLE (Tier 4 lead)].

### I.10 Oral traditions, historical memory and ancestors
- Oral traditions are real and rich but vary by community. Their use as chronology is **DISPUTED**.
- Ancestral memory is maintained through lineage, shrines, funerary rites and masquerade events. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

## Part II — Religion, spirituality and cosmology

> General caution: "Igbo religion" as a single system is a later scholarly construct. Each concept below varies by region and has been reinterpreted since missionary contact.

### II.1 Supreme deity
- **Chukwu** (sometimes Chineke) is recognised across many Igbo-speaking communities as a supreme creator or high god. Whether this concept was pre-colonial and uniformly held, or was shaped by Christian and missionary usage, is **DISPUTED** [MET81; OPA (Tier 4 — Opata; verify)].
- Metuh argues for an indigenous high-god concept with pre-colonial roots, while other scholars stress the role of missionary terminology. Both positions are preserved here.

### II.2 Chi
- **Chi** is a personal spiritual counterpart or guardian, associated with destiny and with the individual's life path. Its exact relationship with the self, with the high god, and with one's agwu is **DISPUTED** and varies by community [MET81; ACH58 for literary usage only].
- The **ikenga** (carved personal object) is associated with achievement and personal drive and is **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** (see Part V and Deliverable B).

### II.3 Ala / Ani
- **Ala** (also Ani) is an earth-deity and moral authority across many communities: linked to land, fertility, ancestry, and the moral order of the community. **CORROBORATED** as a widespread concept with regional variation [COL82; ONW81].
- Ala's shrines and mbari houses (Owerri and Orlu-area communities) are **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC**. Mbari houses are documented in Cole's study [COL82]; their ritual content is **restricted**.

### II.4 Ancestors
- Ancestors (ndị ichie, ndị nna ochie in various usages) were remembered through lineage shrines, compound and obi structures, and funerary rites. **CORROBORATED** across regions, with variation in form and ritual [BAS21; UCH65].

### II.5 Afterlife and the spirit world
- Concepts of the spirit world (ala mmuo, ala of the dead and the spirit realm) and of a return to the ancestral world vary by community. Some communities described a journey or the ancestral world under the earth. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC and DISPUTED** in detail.
- Assertions about a single "Igbo land of the dead" with a fixed landscape are **not supported** by the sources reviewed. This is central to the Field of Cloth assessment (Deliverable C).

### II.6 Reincarnation and returning ancestors
- Reincarnation (a dead ancestor's return through a newborn) is widely documented in Igbo naming and lineage practice. **CORROBORATED** as a concept, with varied interpretations [BAS21; ACH58].
- Ogbanje children (see I.9) are a distinct category of repeated death and return. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Masquerade events in some communities involve the return of the dead, e.g., Odo/Omabe (Nsukka area) and mmonwu (central area) [ONY (verify)]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC and restricted.**

### II.7 Deities and spiritual forces
- **Amadioha:** a thunder and justice deity documented in several south-eastern communities, associated with oath-taking and the white ram and red colour in some accounts. Regional variations are **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [AMA-DIOHA — Tier 4 leads; verify against Metuh and local studies].
- **Idemili:** a river and water deity in Onitsha-area and Anambra communities. Not researched in depth in this package. **GAP.**
- **Agwu:** the deity of divination and healing, associated with the dibia. **CORROBORATED** across several sources, but the anchoring scholarly sources were not located. Current support rests on Tier 4 leads [TIA (Tianmu); CHA (ChatAfrik); NWA (dibia study blog)].
- **Ikenga:** a personal carved object tied to achievement. See Part V and Deliverable B.

### II.8 Divination, healers and religious authority
- **Dibia** (diviner-healers; dibia afa and dibia ogwu are common distinctions) are documented across the region. **CORROBORATED.** Their power is widely described as capable of both healing and harm, and their trustworthiness is a recognised social tension [TIA; CHA — Tier 4 leads].
- Priests of specific shrines (e.g., Ibini Ukpabi of the Aro) and ozo-holders hold ritual authority. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Divination practices are **restricted**; published detail should not be treated as a licence to reproduce them.

### II.9 Sacred spaces, shrines, groves and ritual landscapes
- **Sacred trees:** white cloth wrapped around a tree trunk is reported in some Igbo localities. The one retrieved article that describes it (Okeke 2022, citing Adibe 2008) names two trees with this statement: *Akpu* (silk cotton, *Ceiba pentandra*) and *Udara* (*Chrysophyllum albidum*). The same article describes other sacred trees (Oji, Achi, Iroko, Ngwu, Uha, Ube, Aboshi, Ofo) without any cloth statement. The practice is devotional, not funerary, and REGIONALLY SPECIFIC. The primary source has not been checked. [OKE22; ADI08]
- **Ogirishi tree:** A secondary source reports an Ogirishi tree associated with grave headsides and boundaries; the original source and locality remain unverified. Okeke (2022, Ochendo) states that the graves of men who die unmarried are symbolised by the tree 'holding' a kitchen knife, citing Oha (2019), a blog. Not an established Igbo grave marker. [OKE22; OHA19]
- **Shrine walls:** in the Awka area, sculptures of gods and their supporters are arranged against a shrine wall "often hung with cult apparatus" [COA84, p.89]. This is **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** (Awka), and the apparatus is not described as strips on poles.
- **Obu / obi / ovu / obiri** (ancestral and meeting halls) are named differently across communities [OZI25 — Tier 4 lead]. Their architecture is **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC**.
- **Groves and ritual landscapes:** many communities maintained sacred groves. Their use and restriction vary. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

### II.10 Taboos, purification and consequences
- Taboos (nso) and purification rites are documented across the region; they often concern deaths, twins, the earth, and the shrine. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- The concept of nso ani (taboo against the earth) is documented in the Onitsha context and related to umu ejime and disposal practices [ETH-ONI]. **DISPUTED** as to how much of the disposal practice was real versus missionary construction.

### II.11 Moral order, justice and community obligations
- Ala and ancestral authority enforced moral order; oaths, shrines, elders and secret societies were mechanisms of justice. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Amadioha was invoked in oath-taking in some communities. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

### II.12 Physical landscapes and belief
- Landscapes including groves, rivers, hills, and the earth itself carried spiritual meaning. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** No source reviewed identifies a single, shared sacred landscape type of "fields of hanging objects".

## Part III — Death, funerary practices and the ancestral world
> The full treatment is in **Deliverable B**. Summary below.

- **Death classes:** ordinary death, respected death (titled elder), premature death, and "bad death" (ajo ofia) are distinguished in several communities. Bad deaths include suicide, some infectious deaths, and deaths associated with particular taboos. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [BJM25 — VERIFY; BAS21; ACH58 for the trope].
- **Titled men** were buried within their own compound or obi in several communities; the Ozo grave at Obimo (Nsukka area) is reported as dug in two chambers. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC; one lineage of reporting** [UGWU98, via IGI25 and ONE19].
- **Cloth as grave goods:** cloth was measured in fathoms and interred with the dead; the number increased with status (fathom quantities appear in a secondary account of one community; not confirmed in the retrieved 2025 text — VERIFY). **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [IGI25]. Black cloth was used to cover the corpse in that account.
- **Ikenga** may be broken and buried with its owner. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [AFI86, cited via JCRT 2024; SMA (Smarthistory)].
- **Second burial (ikwa ozu / ime ekwa / ili ozu):** a secondary funerary rite that completes the death. Its timing varies (Nawfia: roughly one year; Igbo Ukwu and other communities differ). Basden noted that "second burial" is a European term [BAS21; BAS66 p.114 via BJM25; UGW (ajol); EZZ (Igwebuike) — VERIFY].
- **Grave construction:** some communities described grave lining with wooden planks (blog-tier lead, not yet verified) [REM (remembersgroup)]. **Unverified** — do not use as a fact.

## Part IV — Clothing, textiles and material culture

### IV.1 Pre-colonial clothing
- Men and women wore wrappers and loincloths of locally woven or dyed cloth; body adornment with beads, coral and metal was common among those with means. **CORROBORATED** across early accounts (Equiano; Basden; Thomas) [EQU89; BAS21; NT13].
- **Equiano's 1789 account** describes both sexes wearing blue-dyed wrappers and red-earth compound walls. Its authenticity as an eyewitness account is **DISPUTED** (Carretta's authorship debate versus Lovejoy and Acholonu) [EQU89; CAR05; ACH89 — VERIFY]. Use with caution as a source for dress.
- **Pre-colonial weaving:** the narrow-strip form is reported only in a Tier 4 lead (AKW) and is not established by the retrieved SPR22 text, which covers two samples. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC; UNVERIFIED.** [AKW; SPR22]

### IV.2 Textiles and dyes
- **Indigo** blue dye and **camwood** red colouring are documented in several accounts. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** in use.
- **Raffia and bast:** wider use in pre-colonial Igbo textiles is not established in the retrieved sources. The two examined Igbo-Ukwu samples are *Ficus* bast and *Raphia* leaf fibre (McIntosh & Cartwright 2022), a finding that applies to those samples only. **Wider use: UNVERIFIED.** [SPR22]
- **Silk: NOT CONFIRMED.** No Igbo textile of pre-colonial date has been identified as silk in the sources reviewed. Do not represent silk.

### IV.3 Funerary and status clothing
- Funerary cloth was distinguished by quality, number and colour, and was part of grave goods (see Part III). **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [IGI25].
- Widowhood dress and mourning restrictions (e.g., shaving, restricted dress for months, ritual washing) are documented in **contemporary** and early-colonial accounts; their pre-colonial form and their regional variation are **DISPUTED** [IRB00 (Canadian IRB document on Nigerian widowhood practices — not Igbo-specific); MAD16 (Tier 4 blog); KAL90 (Ijaw/Kalabari, not Igbo)]. **Do not generalise.**

### IV.4 Beads, jewellery and ornaments
- Beads, coral and metal ornaments (including bronze pieces at Igbo-Ukwu) are documented. **DOCUMENTED** for Igbo-Ukwu [SHA70]. Other regional details require verification.

### IV.5 Pottery, baskets, woodwork, metalwork
- **Metalwork:** iron, copper and bronze working are well documented; Igbo-Ukwu and Awka are key sites. **DOCUMENTED** [SHA70; NEA79].
- **Woodwork and carving:** ikenga, ancestral figures, ozo staffs and shrine figures. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- **Pottery and baskets:** documented in ethnographic and archaeological contexts; detail beyond general presence was not extracted in this package. **GAP.**
- **Ohafia war objects:** the oyaya skull basket and related items are part of Ohafia dance performance, documented in Tier 4 leads [OHA]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** and **restricted** in parts.

### IV.6 Weapons, tools and furniture
- Hand tools, hoes, cutlasses and spears are documented. Stools and studded stools are documented at Igbo-Ukwu (Igbo Richard) [SHA70]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** in use.

### IV.7 Architecture and settlement
- Compounds of mud and red-earth walls, thatched houses and public halls (obi/obu) are documented. **CORROBORATED** [BAS21; UCH65; EQU89 for red-earth walls].
- Obi-type halls for titled or lineage leaders vary in form. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Community gateways and boundaries (thresholds) were marked by trees and shrines. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

### IV.8 Trees, posts, columns, compounds and thresholds
- **Trees:** sacred trees with white cloth (II.9). **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- **Posts and columns:** carved house posts and shrine figures are documented in several communities. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.** No source reviewed documents free-standing poles carrying cloth strips.
- **Compounds:** a major unit of social and ritual life. **CORROBORATED.**

## Part V — Art, symbols and visual language

### V.1 Sculpture and carving
- Shrine sculptures (e.g., gods and their supporters) are documented in Awka and some other areas; they were made by different artists and replaced over time [COA84, p.89; RE-ENT (Re:Entanglements)]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Carved ancestral and ikenga figures are documented. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- The Igbo-Ukwu bronzes are **DOCUMENTED** [SHA70].

### V.2 Uli
- **Uli** is a body and wall painting tradition in parts of the region, with pigments named in ethnographic accounts (including nzu, edo, ufie and oji). Colour assignments should be checked against Northcote Thomas's records [NT13; RE-ENT]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Uli was used on bodies and walls and on shrine walls. Its meanings are **DISPUTED** in detail and vary by community.
- The earliest documented uli-related photographs are Northcote Thomas's 1911 Nibo shrine images [RE-ENT] (Tier 3).

### V.3 Masks and masquerades
- **Ogo** (Afikpo) is a male initiation institution in which dancers' everyday identity is radically erased; Ottenberg documents this in detail [OTT75; OTT89]. Interpretive claims (psychoanalytic) are contested [MAR81 — VERIFY].
- **Odo/Omabe** (Nsukka area): masquerades representing returning dead; **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [ONY — verify].
- **Okoroshi** (Owerri area) and **mmonwu** (central area): masquerade institutions, **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC**.
- All masquerade institutions are **restricted**: women and the uninitiated often may not see certain performances or objects.

### V.4 Colour, materials, patterns and shapes
- Colour and material meanings vary. White and black are recurrent in mourning and sacred contexts in several communities. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC and DISPUTED** in meaning; treat any single colour meaning as unverified without a community source.
- **Uli** patterns and **nsibidi** signs are symbolic systems. Nsibidi is a **restricted** system documented primarily in Cross River contexts (Ejagham/Ekoi, Efik-Ibibio and Ekpe society); its presence in Igbo-speaking areas is less documented and should be weighted accordingly; its meanings are **DISPUTED** and only partly recorded [MAC09; DAY10; DAY11].

### V.5 Ceremonial objects
- Ozo staffs, ikenga, ichi scarification and title regalia have documented meanings within their communities. These are **restricted**; published descriptions do not license reproduction.
- **Ichi** (scarification marks for ozo initiation) are documented in Nri and related contexts, including Northcote Thomas's photographs [ODE (UNN); NWI (Tier 4)]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** and **restricted**.

### V.6 Architecture and decorative motifs
- Compound walls, obi and shrine decorations are documented. Some decorative motifs in Nri are said to evoke sun rays [ODE (UNN)]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** and not assumed universal.

### V.7 Sacred and restricted imagery
- Ikenga, shrine figures, masquerade costumes, nsibidi symbols, mbari imagery, and ichi marks are all restricted to varying degrees. Reproducing them requires consultation.

### V.8 Representations of ancestors and spirits
- Ancestral and spirit figures are documented in shrine and masquerade contexts. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC and restricted.**
- Figures from Igbo-Ukwu are archaeological; their identity and function are **DISPUTED**.

### V.9 Historical forms of memorialisation
- Memorial practice includes shrines, ancestral halls, second burial, mbari houses (Owerri), and the sacred tree with white cloth. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Nsude's ten circular stepped mud pyramids dedicated to the Uto deity were photographed by G.I. Jones in the 1930s; reports say they were destroyed around 1962 [JON84; MAA (Tier 3)]. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC**; they bear no cloth, which matters for the Field of Cloth assessment.

### V.10 Traditional versus contemporary
- Contemporary Igbo art, including highlife and the modern use of uli and ikenga motifs, is a separate category from pre-colonial practice. Contemporary reinterpretation must be labelled as such. [CONTEMP]

## Part VI — Social customs and everyday life

### VI.1 Household organisation
- Compounds housed extended families; wives and children had defined roles. **CORROBORATED** [UCH65; BAS21].

### VI.2 Food and domestic activity
- Yam, cassava (post-colonial in widespread use), cocoyam, palm products and game were staples in many communities; food restrictions (nso) varied. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

### VI.3 Markets and trade
- The four-day market week (see I.7). **CORROBORATED.**
- Long-distance trade through the Aro network, and local market exchange. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

### VI.4 Farming and harvest
- Yam cultivation dominates the agricultural calendar; new yam festivals are celebrated in many communities. **CORROBORATED** with regional variation [UCH65; AHI (Tier 4)].

### VI.5 Blacksmithing and specialised crafts
- Awka smiths, itinerant smiths, and the Lejja smelting evidence (I.2). **DOCUMENTED** [NEA79; LEJ24].
- Ogene bells are associated with Awka. **CORROBORATED** as a musical instrument (I.7); its origin is linked to iron working in some accounts [GAR20 — Tier 4].

### VI.6 Healers
- Dibia afa and dibia ogwu. See II.8. **CORROBORATED.**

### VI.7 Music, dance and oral performance
- Ogene (iron bell), ekwe (slit drum), udu (pot drum), igba (membrane drum), ichaka (basket rattle), oja (wooden whistle) and ubo aka (thumb piano) are documented; ogene is performed at marriage, praise, funerals and other events. **CORROBORATED** [GAR20; MEL (Tier 4); AKA (Tier 4)].
- Ekwe is used to summon assemblies in some communities. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Atilogwu and masquerade (mmanwu/mmonwu) orchestration are documented in later and contemporary contexts. **COL-DEV / CONTEMP.**

### VI.8 Festivals and ceremonies
- New yam festivals, masquerade seasons, and title ceremonies. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- The Ahiajoku festival (Owerri-associated, mid-20th century) is a modern institution; its pre-colonial status is **INFERRED** only. [AHI; COL-DEV]

### VI.9 Childhood, education and initiation
- Initiation rites exist in many communities (e.g., ogo in Afikpo, ichi for ozo). **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** and **restricted** [OTT75; OTT89].
- Childhood naming and ancestor-linked naming are documented. **CORROBORATED.**

### VI.10 Community gatherings and social responsibilities
- Lineage meetings, village assemblies, the obi/obu and market gatherings. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Women's associations (umuada) and age grades. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**

### VI.11 Gender roles
- Gender roles varied greatly: some communities allowed women significant title-holding, lineage power and market authority (e.g., Omu in Onitsha-area; umuada). Others restricted women from many rites. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** [AMA87; ILO (Tier 2 lead)].
- Amadiume's argument about gender flexibility in Nnobi is a major and **DISPUTED** interpretation [AMA87].

### VI.12 Conflict, justice, punishment and reconciliation
- Elders' councils, oath-taking, shrine oracles and, in some communities, the Aro oracle system. **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC.**
- Punishment for certain offences included banishment and, in some accounts, sacrifice. **DISPUTED** in extent and documentation.

## Part VII — Historical misconceptions and colonial distortions

### VII.1 Sources and their authors
- **Colonial officers and anthropologists:** Basden (missionary and writer), Talbot (district officer), Northcote Thomas (government anthropologist), G.I. Jones (colonial officer), Macgregor and Dayrell (nsibidi). Their records are primary but shaped by colonial purposes. **Use with corroboration.**
- **Missionaries:** the "demon superstition" framing of twins and some other practices was partly a missionary and colonial construction [ETH-ONI].
- **Later scholarship:** Afigbo (1972) critiqued colonial indirect rule and the acephalous model [AFI72]. Henderson (1972), Onwuejeogwu (1981), Amadiume (1987), Isichei (1976) and Uchendu (1965) revise colonial pictures.

### VII.2 Incorrect generalisations
- "All Igbo are acephalous" — **DISPUTED** and not supported in its general form.
- "All Igbo bury in the compound / in communal cemeteries" — **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC** (IGI25 describes titled men's compound burial).
- "Igbo twin killing was universal" — **DISPUTED**; the trope has been critiqued [ETH-ONI].
- "Igbo funerary cloth hangs on poles" — **NOT SUPPORTED** in the sources reviewed (see Deliverable B, §B.7).

### VII.3 Misinterpreted spiritual practices
- Ikenga, ozo, ichi and masquerades have been misread as generic "tribal fetish" objects in colonial writing. **DOCUMENTED** as a colonial pattern.
- Human sacrifice claims in some colonial accounts are **DISPUTED** and vary in reliability.

### VII.4 Suppressed or transformed practices
- Twin infanticide (criminalised in some areas), some masquerade forms, and some title ceremonies were suppressed or changed under missionary and colonial rule. **DOCUMENTED** in general terms; specific histories are **REGIONALLY SPECIFIC**.

### VII.5 Modern popular representations
- Popular images of Igbo life (e.g., generic "African" drums, masks, and funerary cloth) often lack reliable historical evidence. **DOCUMENTED** pattern.
- Many online summaries rely on other summaries. Tier 4 sources are used in this package only as leads.

### VII.6 Academic evidence versus popular claims
- Academic work distinguishes documented practices, regional variations and interpretations; popular work often flattens them. The Field of Cloth concept sits closer to the popular-image category unless grounded in a specific community practice.
